Electrical & instrumentation

Inside electrical & instrumentation

By M&M EngineeringWindhoek5 min read

Ask a developer what makes a building work and they will talk about structure and finishes. Ask the people who run the building and they will talk about the electrical and instrumentation scope. It is the difference between a building that stands and a building that behaves.

What the scope actually covers

On our services page the E&I list has nine lines. Read together, they describe the nervous system of any serious facility:

  • Supply and erection of major electrical equipment: transformers, switchgear, generators and the heavy apparatus that connects a facility to power.
  • Power distribution systems: getting electricity from the intake to every panel, safely and measurably.
  • Lighting and power: the everyday layer that occupants touch.
  • Fire detection and alarm systems: the systems nobody hopes to use, which must work the one time they are needed.
  • Programmable logic control (PLC): the small computers that run plant and processes automatically.
  • SCADA systems: the supervisory layer that lets operators see and control everything above.
  • Structural cabling: the structured network cabling that every other system rides on.
  • Security systems: access control, surveillance and the electronics of a secure perimeter.
  • Instrumentation and control works: the sensors and loops that make processes measurable and adjustable.

Why the list hangs together

Each of these systems is easy to buy and hard to integrate. The fire alarm must talk to the HVAC. The PLC data must reach the SCADA screen. The cabling must be there before the equipment that uses it is ordered, not after. When one contractor is responsible for the whole E&I scope, integration stops being everyone's problem and becomes one company's deliverable.

What a client should ask for

Three things: an E&I partner who manages its own design sub-contractors, including specialised studies where the risk demands them; documentation that matches what was actually installed; and testing that is witnessed, not asserted. Our engineering team manages the requirements and performance of design sub-contractors for exactly this reason, and our commissioning culture assumes the standard is the standard.

Buildings stand on concrete. They behave on E&I.
